If frontier AI labs are becoming the next generation of biotech platforms, how should we position?

Frontier AI labs are buying biotechs, building wet labs and starting their own drug programmes. Design gets cheap and validation stays scarce, so the physical layer gets paid: molecule makers, lab tools, testing services and data owners.

Tickers: TWST, OABI, DHR, WAT, RVTY, TXG, ILMN, TMO, A, ICLR, CRL, 2395.T, GENB, LLY, NTRA, TEM

The frontier AI labs are changing what they are. They are no longer just selling models to drug companies. They are buying biotechs, building wet labs and starting their own drug programmes. That puts them in competition with pharma and makes them buyers of everything a drug company buys. This paper asks who gets paid when that happens, and what we should own.

1. AI Drug Discovery Theme

Frontier AI labs are turning themselves into biotech platforms. Three signals point the same way:

  • Frontier labs are buying their way into biology. Anthropic has bought a drug programme, built a wet lab and begun drug discovery of its own. OpenAI, Google's Isomorphic Labs and NVIDIA are moving the same way.

  • The supply chain reports the volume. Twist's AI orders, GenScript's capacity and ICON's book-to-bill all show it.

  • The economics favour the physical layer. Design gets cheap, and validation stays scarce. Monkey are getting expensive everywhere.

Our first AI-science primer sketched which suppliers could benefit. This version goes further. We read the news and blog posts of every frontier lab to separate announced partners from likely candidates that may have something in the works:

  • Announced: Twist, Danaher, Waters, Revvity, 10x, ICON, Generate and Tecan (Anthropic), Thermo Fisher and Ginkgo (OpenAI), Lilly (Isomorphic and OpenAI).

  • Candidates: Charles River, SNBL, Agilent, Illumina, Bruker, Natera, Tempus and OmniAb.

We also want to mentioned that:

  • AI has not yet proven it makes better drugs. AI-designed molecules pass Phase 1 far more often, but they fail Phase 2 at the usual rate. The final verdict comes with Phase II data in 2028-30.

  • The losers are in the same sector. Design software and small AI biotechs face a competitor with better models and deeper pockets. Big pharma, led by Lilly, is building its own platforms. People building the models agree. Retro Bio's head of applied AI says protein models are multiplying rather than getting better, and that "we probably don't need another protein folding model".

  • The labs may not be the ultimate beneficiary. Dario Amodei is a biophysicist by training (Princeton PhD on neural circuits), and Anthropic can afford patience. Coding and white-collar automation pay the bills. He has said AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs, and has floated a 3% tax on AI revenue as the vehicle to ease the transition. Biology can run at a loss for years on that revenue. That is why we own the suppliers now rather than wait for the labs' own drugs to pay off.

Own the theme: yes. Own all 21 names: no.

2. Why now: the sentence buried in Anthropic's 27 October post

Not because biotech stocks moved. Because a frontier AI lab told the world it is no longer just selling models to drug companies. It is becoming one.

On 27 October Anthropic published early results from its drug-discovery work with outside partners. The headline was six partnerships, with BigHat, Bristol-Myers Squibb, Divergence, Enveda, Generate Biomedicines and The Column Group. The more important sentence came near the end: "We've also begun drug discovery efforts of our own. We plan to scale this work up substantially from here."

Read alone, that is a throwaway line. Read alongside what Anthropic has done this year, it describes a strategy:

Anthropic's biology build-out, 2026 · 5 building blocks · company posts, Reuters, TechCrunch

The drug step is the one that matters. Mithril, essentially a one-person company, reached mouse data for a rare muscular dystrophy (LAMA2) with no lab of its own. The lab result is still a preprint, humans ran the bench work, and Anthropic says the lab is not specifically a drug-discovery lab.

Put together, this is the outline of a biotech platform: models that propose molecules, a lab that tests them, a pipeline of its own, and partners to take the winners through human trials.

Why would a model company want its own drugs? Three reasons stand out:

  1. Data creation and retention. Every experiment a lab runs on its own programmes also trains its next model (section 5).

  2. Early monetisation through licensing pre-clinical work. There is already a mature market for early-stage drug assets.

  3. If frontier model design has higher success rate in Phase II trial than human designs, this will worth a great deal. Biotech has always been hit-or-miss. If AI makes discovery repeatable, and that is still a very big if, the value created is far larger than selling software seats (such as co-work and codex).

Use at the bench is already real. Retro Bio says Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex now do much of its lab's hypothesis generation and analysis. Scientists with no coding background can run 80-90% of its computational workflows, and productivity is up 10-20% broadly and 30-50% in some areas. These are one lab's own estimates, not measured data.

3. It's not just Anthropic

Every major AI lab is moving into biology. The trend is under-covered because most of the important players are private.

Anthropic is the most aggressive so far. It is the only lab with documented evidence of all three of its own drug programmes, its own wet lab and biotech acquisitions. But it is not alone, and the others are moving in the same direction from different starting points.

Frontier labs' biology moves, Jan-Oct 2026 · 15 dated events · company posts, filings and press (sources in section 13)

The labs differ in how far they have gone:

  • Anthropic is building a vertically integrated biotech: models, a wet lab, instrument control, acquired programmes and partners for trials.

  • OpenAI is building the model and the commercial layer. It has a specialist life-sciences model, equity stakes in design startups, workflow software, and a CFO who has said openly that OpenAI wants a share of the value of what its models discover, not just a subscription fee. Its clearest result is with Retro Bio (private). GPT-4b micro, a protein model trained with OpenAI, redesigned two of the four Yamanaka reprogramming factors and made them about 50x more efficient at turning cells into stem cells. It took a couple of months, against about 10 years for the earlier academic effort.

  • Google's Isomorphic Labs, run by Demis Hassabis, is closest to a real drug pipeline, with three large pharma partners. It has not yet dosed a human. First-in-human was targeted for end-2025 and is now expected around end-2026. (this could be a catalyst for this theme).

  • NVIDIA sells the toolkit to everyone. BioNeMo is the one platform that names both Anthropic and OpenAI as users, and NVIDIA's venture arm holds small stakes in Generate, Recursion and Lila (two listed AI-drug biotechs, GENB and RXRX, and Lila Sciences, a private autonomous-lab startup).

  • Microsoft and Meta are behind. Microsoft sells a research platform but runs no drug programmes. Meta's protein-model team left to form EvolutionaryScale and later moved to the Chan Zuckerberg Biohub. We found no biology activity at xAI.

4. The 101: how a drug actually gets made

To see where AI changes the economics, and where it doesn't, you have to see the whole journey. It takes 10 to 15 years, and most of the money is spent at the end.

A new medicine passes through five stages, and at each one a different set of companies gets paid.

  1. Discovery (2-4 years). Scientists pick a target, usually a protein involved in a disease, and design molecules that might act on it. Each design has to be physically made, which is where DNA writers like Twist come in, then measured with lab instruments from Danaher, Thermo Fisher, Agilent, Waters and Bruker. This is the stage AI is transforming.

  2. Preclinical (1-2 years). The best candidates are tested for safety in cells and then in animals, often monkeys for antibody drugs, so regulators will allow a first test in people. Charles River and SNBL do much of this work.

  3. Phase 1 (1-2 years). A small group of healthy volunteers or patients. The question is safety and dosing.

  4. Phase 2 (2-3 years). A few hundred patients. The question is whether it works. This is where most drugs die.

  5. Phase 3 (2-4 years), then FDA review (about 1 year). Thousands of patients across many hospitals, run largely by contract research organisations such as ICON. Phase 3 alone is about three-quarters of trial cost.

Third-party life-sciences sector primer, 8 Jul 2026: stage times p3, attrition p4 (read from its chart, approximate), cost p4

The chart makes the central point. Discovery is the cheaper part. Discovery and preclinical work are 10-30% of the cost of a drug, depending on how failures are counted. The standard reference, a Tufts study (DiMasi et al., 2016), puts it at about 30%: $430m of $1.4bn out-of-pocket per approved drug. The rest, the human trials and regulatory work, is largely untouched by AI today.

The early evidence on AI-designed drugs fits this picture. Since 2019 about $60bn has been invested in AI drug discovery, and about 175 AI-derived programmes have reached human trials. In Phase 1, AI-designed molecules have passed 80-90% of the time against about 50% historically. In Phase 2, the stage that decides whether a drug works, they pass about 40% of the time, the same as everyone else. As of mid-2026, no fully AI-designed drug has been approved.

What this means for investors: AI makes better-behaved molecules, faster. It has not yet shown it makes drugs that work better in patients. Though this is what many frontier labs are trying to change. Until it does, the money is in the first two stages, where the volume of experiments is exploding, and in whoever can run more trials. A bet on the final verdict is a bet on 2028-30 Phase II data.

5. One framework: the value-chain map

When AI makes proposing a molecule almost free, the scarce resource moves to whoever makes, measures and tests it. The labs cannot do all of that themselves, so they buy it. Below, the 21 names are grouped into six buckets by what they actually sell.

This changes what a lab is selling. Traditional drug discovery asks, "Does candidate X work?" AI drug discovery also asks, "What can this experiment teach the model?" Failures count as much as successes: of 1,320 Claude-designed binders tested, 354 worked, and the other 966 became training data. In some workflows the customer cares less about the physical protein than about the structured results flowing back into the model. The labs that make and test molecules start to look like data foundries.

Owning that data is affordable for a lab. The Protein Data Bank cost about $20bn to build (200,000 structures at about $100,000 each), but OpenFold matched AlphaFold2 using about 1% of it. A well-designed private dataset therefore costs hundreds of millions, not billions.

1. Molecule makers

These companies turn a digital design into a physical molecule: DNA, proteins or antibodies, made to order and paid per order. They see the volume first. Twist is Anthropic's DNA supplier. Ginkgo runs OpenAI's autonomous-lab experiments. OmniAb's engineered animals, including OmniChicken, make fully human antibodies.

Evidence. The volume is already in suppliers' numbers, and in the right way: volume up with margins holding or rising, the sign of a supplier keeping the gains rather than cutting price. Twist's case is in its write-up below. The clearest outside read is GenScript, the largest Chinese peer. Its AI drug-discovery business doubled year on year in the first half of 2026, its Life Science Group grew 28.8%, and adjusted gross margin rose to 57.8%. Industry checks put its validation capacity at about 8,000 designs a day, heading for 16,000 by year end.

We exclude GenScript on China risk. US policy is splitting the supply chain: the BIOSECURE Act (signed 18 December 2025) bars federal money from Chinese "biotechnology companies of concern", and WuXi AppTec joined the Pentagon's 1260H list in June 2026. We expect US drug-discovery teams to stop using Chinese labs, and the split to run both ways over time. That pushes more volume to US and allied suppliers.

Customers send a digital sequence, and Twist sends back physical DNA, or increasingly the proteins and test data made from it. It is paid per order, so it is a toll on every experiment.

Why it fits. Every protein a model designs starts as a DNA sequence that someone has to make. Twist is Anthropic's DNA supplier in three separate research posts. It is the only listed company that has filed an 8-K about the relationship (19 August, filed as a fair-disclosure update, not a material contract). It also supplies Lilly's TuneLab ecosystem and is quoted in Google DeepMind's SynthID Bio launch. Twist serves every lab. AI teams increasingly run pooled screens built for models rather than people: about a million designed proteins, each tagged with a DNA barcode, tested together and read out in one sequencing run. Every such screen starts with a DNA order.

The evidence. Twist guides to triple-digit percentage growth in AI drug-discovery orders in fiscal 2026, and another year of triple-digit growth in fiscal 2027. Revenue over the last 12 months was $432m. Its synthesis line grew 39% in the first half, faster than the company.

The catch. AI orders are still a minority of revenue. Twist does not disclose them in dollars. At 22x EV/sales the market is pricing the AI ramp as certain. The CEO sold about $92m of stock between August and October, and the company raised more than $300m at $96 in August. The stock is up 378% in a year, and at least one known short seller has published against it.

Competition. Twist is not the only supplier, and the short case leans on this:

  • Rival DNA makers. Danaher's IDT, Thermo Fisher's GeneArt, Azenta's Genewiz and GenScript all sell DNA to order. Private enzymatic-synthesis startups (Ansa, DNA Script, Elegen) are attacking cost and gene length.

  • Anthropic uses others too. Its 1,320-binder test ran through Adaptyv Bio, not Twist.

  • The short report. Scorpion Capital (19 Aug) argues Anthropic describes Twist as an "independent contract research organization", one vendor among several. It notes the 8-K was a fair-disclosure filing (Item 7.01), not a material contract (Item 1.01).

Our read: Twist's edge is scale, speed and price on complex genes, not exclusivity. The Anthropic link is real but not locked in. Two of the rivals sit inside basket names (IDT at Danaher, GeneArt at Thermo Fisher), so the basket keeps the DNA-synthesis exposure even if Twist loses share.

Verdict: bullish, the core of the basket. It has the clearest documented link, the only reported AI order numbers, and it sells to every lab. The 12% fall on 30 October puts it 30% below its high, the first pullback since the Anthropic posts began. We would start a position now and add after the 12 November results if the fiscal 2027 AI-order guide holds.

2. Lab instruments and consumables

These companies sell the equipment and supplies every lab runs on. Most revenue is recurring consumables and service. This is where the frontier labs are physically plugging in. Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard (27 August) lets Claude drive instruments directly, with Tecan, Danaher and QIAGEN at launch and Waters from October. Revvity and 10x have official Claude connectors. Thermo Fisher is a named customer of OpenAI's GPT-Rosalind. A neutral supplier wins whichever lab wins. Anthropic building its own wet lab helps this bucket, because that lab buys the same instruments.

3. Testing services

Contract research organisations (CROs) run studies for drug companies, for a fee. Charles River and SNBL run the animal safety studies required before a drug enters people. ICON runs human trials. This bucket faces two tensions:

  • Volume vs. price. More AI-designed candidates means more studies. But faster, AI-run trials mean fewer billable hours. ICON is already in "gain-sharing" pricing talks, and Medpace's CEO calls AI a "net negative to a service company".

  • Bottleneck vs. policy. Monkeys are scarce: the US uses 60,000-70,000 a year, and imports fell 43% in 2023. But the FDA wants animal studies to become "the exception rather than the norm". Its September 2026 rule says it "does not eliminate or prohibit animal studies", so this is a slow headwind, not a ban.

Evidence. Mithril reached mouse data in under 18 months by renting 11 outside labs. One small AI-native programme fed eleven service providers. If we see more one-man design teams using external labs, the demand can go exponential.

10. Risks with this thesis

This is a narrative trade first. The real validation comes with Phase II results from AI-designed drugs, from late 2028 onward. Until then, the stocks trade on announcements, partnerships and order commentary, not on proof that AI makes better medicines. The base rate is sobering. About 10% of drugs entering Phase I reached approval in 2006-15, and 7.9% in 2011-20, straight through the genomics, CRISPR and machine-learning eras. No AI-discovered drug has been approved yet. Retro Bio expects AI drugs to need 5-10 years from first human dose to approval.

Three forces decide whether the money pool grows or is just redistributed, and each can turn against us:

  • China as a cheap supplier of molecules. Chinese biotechs out-licensed about $136bn of assets in 2025, and China-origin assets were over half of global pharma licensing in Q1 2026. Cheap Chinese molecules compete with AI-designed ones. BIOSECURE limits Chinese labs, not Chinese assets.

  • Frontier-lab budgets stay small. Even 1% of an $80bn lab's revenue is only ~$0.8bn a year. If the labs keep biology as a side project, or build capacity in-house, the new demand does not arrive at scale.

Other risks:

  • AI drugs fail at normal rates in Phase II. This is the long-term thesis breaker. If AI-designed drugs look great in discovery and Phase I but fail like everyone else's in Phase II and III, the "repeatable discovery engine" is a faster way to make the same mistakes. Labs would cut experimental budgets, and the experiment volume behind buckets 1-3 would stall. The first real answers arrive in 2028-30.

  • Experimental budgets stop growing. Near term, the supply-chain trade depends on biotech funding and pharma R&D budgets far more than on Anthropic. A funding drought, a rate shock or drug-pricing policy (most-favoured-nation pricing) would hit tools and CROs regardless of AI.

  • Capacity catches up. Bottleneck trades end when supply responds. GenScript is roughly doubling its validation capacity this year. New monkey-breeding facilities, including a proposed 30,000-animal site, would ease the preclinical squeeze.

  • AI gains go to the customer. CROs are already negotiating "gain-sharing". If shorter trials mean fewer billable hours faster than they mean more trials, CRO revenue shrinks while volumes rise.

  • The FDA moves faster on monkeys. A final rule cutting monkey studies for antibodies would hit Charles River and SNBL directly. It is a draft, not a rule, today.

  • The labs insource. Anthropic already has its own wet lab. If frontier labs build more capacity in-house, outsourced service providers lose share, though instrument makers still sell the equipment.

  • Politics around Anthropic. Anthropic's IPO prospectus cites a February presidential order to stop using its models and a Defense Department supply-chain risk designation. It warns of "material revenue losses or business disruptions" and of being "disproportionately or selectively targeted by particular governmental bodies". Government revenue is under 1%, but the risk reaches partners. This favours suppliers that serve every lab, OpenAI's and Google's included.

  • Insiders are selling into the rally. Twist's CEO sold about $92m between August and October, and the company raised more than $300m at $96 in August (the stock is now about $153). Tempus's CEO sold about $77m. Recursion raised $200m at $3.25. When management sells at these prices, we should ask what they see.

  • It is one crowded trade. The tools and CRO names rallied about 20% before this paper, mostly on higher multiples rather than higher earnings. Many sit near 52-week highs. A sector rotation would hit the whole basket at once.

Glossary

Term Plain English
Frontier lab A company building the most capable general AI models: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind
Wet lab A physical lab where experiments use real chemicals, cells and animals, not computer simulation
Binder A protein designed to stick to a specific target molecule, the starting point of many drugs
Preclinical Safety and activity testing in cells and animals before a drug is given to people
Phase 1 / 2 / 3 Human trials: safety (small), whether it works (hundreds), confirmation at scale (thousands)
CRO Contract research organisation: runs studies or trials for drug companies, for a fee
NHP Non-human primate, usually cynomolgus monkeys, used in antibody safety studies
NAMs New approach methods: non-animal tests such as human-cell assays, organ chips and computer models
Model Hardware Standard Anthropic's open specification that lets AI models operate lab instruments
MCP / connector A standard plug that lets an AI model read from and write to another company's software
BioNeMo NVIDIA's software toolkit for biology AI, used by most of the labs
Book-to-bill New contracts signed divided by revenue billed. Above 1.0x means the backlog is growing
Federated learning Training a model on several parties' data without the data leaving each owner
