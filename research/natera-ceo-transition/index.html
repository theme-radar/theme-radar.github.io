Natera: Is the Sudden CEO Change a Buying Opportunity?

Buy in two parts, starting at about $312. Natera fell 28% in eight trading days: about half in a market sell-off, the rest after the CEO resigned without a stated reason. Sales grew 34% and cancer test volumes hit a record. At 14.6x sales the price is roughly fair on our numbers (probability-weighted value $315), so the second half waits for $275 or the 26-Feb-27 results.

Tickers: NTRA

Decision

Buy, in two parts, starting now at about $312. Buy the first half now. Buy the second half at $275, or after the 26-Feb-27 results if growth holds.

Why

  • The business is growing fast and beat sales expectations again.
  • The stock fell 28% in eight trading days. About half of that fall came in a market sell-off before any company news and the rest followed the sudden CEO change.
  • The one real question is the sudden CEO change. Nothing in the filings or the results points to a problem behind it.

What could go wrong

  • A bad reason for the CEO's exit comes out, or another top executive leaves.
  • The stock is still not cheap. It trades at 14.6 times sales against its own five-year average of about 8.9. On our numbers today's price is roughly fair, not a bargain.
  • The holdings report from Duquesne (Stanley Druckenmiller's family office) is due by 16-Nov. Natera was its largest position at 30-Jun. If it has been cut, the stock could fall further.

The key issue

The CEO of almost eight years stepped down on 5-Nov with immediate effect and gave no reason. Everything else at the company looks on track.

Did it beat expectations? On sales, yes. Sales were $794m against about $708m expected, and the full-year sales forecast was raised by $140m to about $3.02bn. On profit, no. The loss was $0.42 a share against $0.28 expected, because the company chose to spend more.

Does anything in the results say otherwise? Two things to keep an eye on. Neither breaks the growth story.

  • Spending. The higher sales forecast adds about $91m of gross profit, but planned spending rose by $112.5m. Spending is set to grow another 10–12% next year.
  • Cash collection. Money owed by insurers rose 59% since December, faster than sales. Free cash flow was only $14.7m in nine months.

What do we know about the CEO change?

  • Known. Steve Chapman resigned as CEO and director, effective 5-Nov. The company's filing says there was no disagreement. Solomon Moshkevich took over the same day. He has been at Natera 15 years and built its cancer business.
  • Not known. The actual reason. The company says only that it was “the right time”. Nobody has been named to fill Moshkevich's old job.

The business in four lines

  • Natera runs blood tests that read DNA. They are used in pregnancy screening, to check transplanted organs, and to check whether a cancer has come back.
  • Health insurers pay, not patients. The US government's Medicare programme is about 17% of sales.
  • The cancer test, Signatera, is about half of sales on our estimate and nearly all of the growth.
  • Natera collects about $1,290 per cancer test on average. When an insurer pays in full it gets more than $3,000. As more insurers agree to cover the test, the average rises at almost no extra cost. That is the main source of future profit.

What drives growth

Cancer test volume is the main driver. In our base case, sales rise by about $722m next year, from $3.06bn to $3.78bn. The $3.06bn is our figure for 2026, slightly above the company's $3.02bn forecast. More cancer tests explain about 85% of that. These are our estimates, because Natera does not report sales by product.

Profit follows sales, and spending decides how much is kept. The operating loss narrows from about $261m this year to about break-even in 2027. Gross profit rises by about $501m. Higher spending on research and overheads takes back about $244m. A higher price per test matters most for profit, because it costs almost nothing to deliver.

What has driven the recent increase

Cancer tests grew 56% on a year earlier last quarter. It is not advertising. A patient cannot buy this test. A cancer doctor has to order it. Three things get the doctor to order:

  1. Sales reps. Natera built up its cancer sales team in 2025. In 2026 the number of productive reps was held flat and tests kept growing. More than half of US cancer doctors ordered the test in the last quarter of 2025.
  2. Clinical evidence. A doctor wants proof that patients do better. Natera has opened more than 70 studies since 2019 and they are now reporting results. Good results get the test written into treatment guidelines, which doctors follow.
  3. Insurance coverage. Doctors avoid tests that leave patients with a bill. Each time Medicare covers another cancer type, more doctors order. Liver cancer was added this month and about 10 more requests are pending.

Sources: Q3-26 call, Q4-25 call, Wolfe conference, 10-Nov-26.

Why the evidence is a moat that compounds

  • It takes years and cannot be bought. A study has to enrol patients and then wait to see whether the cancer returns. Natera already has five-year results. A rival starting today is five years behind, and by then Natera will have ten-year results.
  • Each step feeds the next. Evidence leads to guidelines. Guidelines lead to insurance coverage. Coverage leads to more doctors ordering. More patients tested means more data for the next study.
  • Patients repeat. Once a patient starts, they are retested about every three months, roughly 10 tests over five years. This year's new patients keep producing tests in 2027 and 2028 without new selling.
  • Doctors widen their use. A doctor who sees it work in one cancer type starts using it in others.

How AI helps

AI cannot shortcut this moat. It can speed up writing and analysis, but not the years of patient follow-up that a study needs. That protects Natera's lead. AI also adds four tailwinds:

  • Its patient data becomes more valuable. AI drug developers need large sets of real patient data over time. Natera has tested more than 500,000 cancer patients. Pharma and data sales were $63m in nine months against $34m in all of 2025. Management expects several hundred million dollars a year by 2029. Its pregnancy-test data has not been commercialised yet.
  • Its own AI models. Natera launched its own models in August 2025 and partnered with NVIDIA in January 2026. The company reports analysis running about 76% faster and 59% cheaper in early tests. These are its own figures.
  • Getting paid. AI tools chase insurers for payment on covered tests. Management names this as one reason the price per test keeps rising.
  • More drug trials. If AI produces more drug candidates, more trials need a test like Natera's to measure results. Natera has announced seven new pharma partnerships since May.

Two limits to keep in view. Competitors get the same AI tools, so Natera's edge is its data and evidence, not the software. And the data business is still only about 3% of sales.

What this means. The growth engine rests on evidence, coverage and a base of repeat patients that took years to build. None of that leaves with a CEO. That is why Natera is a business worth owning through this change.
