Channel check: what an mRNA lab at Penn told us about AI, monkeys and the wet lab

A Penn mRNA scientist confirmed that monkeys, wet-lab capacity and labour are the scarce inputs in drug discovery, and flagged two risks: AI still needs heavy human checking, and China is now the cheapest source of lab kit.

Tickers: CRL, 2395.T, ICLR, TWST, DHR, TMO, TECN, WAT, A, BRKR, RVTY, DNA, GENB, RXRX, CERT, NTRA, TEM
Tracked in: AI Science basket

Talk hosted by the Penn Club of Thailand, Bangkok, 3 Nov 2026

Bottom line. A working scientist from the lab behind the mRNA vaccines confirmed three of our core hypotheses: monkeys are expensive, wet-lab capacity is short, and labour is the biggest cost in discovery. She also raised two things we under-weight. AI output still needs heavy human checking, and China is now the cheapest source of lab materials and machines. The first supports the molecule makers, lab-tool makers and testing labs. The second is a new pricing risk for the US instrument makers.

Who spoke, and why the lab matters

  • The speaker. Dr Elena Atochina-Vasserman, Research Assistant Professor of Medicine in Dr Drew Weissman's lab at the Penn Institute for RNA Innovation (University of Pennsylvania). She joined the lab in 2018. Her own work is on delivering mRNA medicines to the lung and on an mRNA vaccine for norovirus.
  • The lab. Drew Weissman and Katalin Karikó won the 2023 Nobel Prize in Medicine for the nucleoside base modification that stops the body attacking synthetic mRNA. That discovery is what made the COVID vaccines possible. Penn licensed it, and both BioNTech (with Pfizer) and Moderna built their COVID vaccines on it.
  • The institute. Penn set up the Institute for RNA Innovation in December 2021, with Weissman as director. Its targets include HIV, malaria, influenza, cancer, allergies, autoimmune disease and sickle cell anaemia.
  • Global health. She said she works with the Gates Foundation on HIV and malaria vaccines for Africa. For context, the Gates Foundation committed $40m in 2023 to help African manufacturers (Institut Pasteur de Dakar, Biovac) make low-cost mRNA vaccines.
  • The room. Practitioners from pharma, biotech investing and the life-science industry, plus researchers from Chulalongkorn University.
  • Second voice. Prof. Dr Kiat Ruxrungtham, Director of the Chula Vaccine Research Center at Chulalongkorn University. His centre developed ChulaCov19, Thailand's own mRNA COVID vaccine, with Drew Weissman's lab. Human trials began in June 2021.

What we heard, against our hypotheses

1. Monkeys are expensive. Confirmed.

  • What she said: non-human primate studies are a major cost in getting a drug to the clinic. When we put it at about $33,000 per monkey, she agreed it was in that range.
  • The data agrees. Lab macaques in China cost about 180,000 yuan (about $27,000) each in July 2026, back at pandemic-era highs, and suppliers say colonies are pre-booked.
  • Read-through: pricing power for the animal-safety CROs. CRL sources about 80% of the monkeys it uses from its own breeding, and 2395.T (SNBL) also breeds its own. Scarcity turns into margin for both. The risk to this call is unchanged: the FDA's push to cut monkey testing for antibodies.

2. Wet labs are in short supply. Confirmed.

  • What she said: physical lab capacity is a constraint, not just a cost.
  • Read-through: supports the outsourcing model behind Mithril (the one-founder biotech Anthropic acquired, which ran on 11 rented labs) and demand for CROs (CRL, ICLR). It also supports instrument demand, because new capacity has to be equipped.

3. Labour is the biggest cost in discovery, so autonomous labs are the direction. Confirmed, with a timing caveat.

  • What she said: people are the most expensive input in the discovery phase. Autonomous labs are the future, but first for large pharma and large labs. Nothing more specific on vendors or timing.
  • Read-through: automation spend goes to big buyers first, which favours the large incumbents with automation lines and Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard links: DHR, TMO, TECN, WAT. It does not rescue DNA (Ginkgo), whose problem is selling automation to the varied work of smaller labs.

4. AI is in use, but hallucinations mean humans must check everything. A caution we should weight more.

  • What she said: AI is already used in the lab, but models make things up often enough that results need human verification. Prof. Kiat put a number on it: AI is very helpful, but it hallucinates about 20% of the time, so he checks everything. He did not say which model.
  • This matches a recent Accelerate Bio podcast with Retro Bio's head of applied AI, where AI co-scientists were called "their own worst enemy" for confidently rejecting new ideas.
  • Read-through, two ways. Verification means more physical experiments, which is good for the make-and-test layer (TWST, CRL and the instrument makers). It also slows the "AI replaces the scientist" story, which is the bull case for AI drug developers and design software (GENB, RXRX, CERT).

5. China is the cheapest source of materials and machines, then Korea, then India. New risk for the US instrument makers.

  • What she said: for both consumables and equipment, Chinese suppliers are cheapest, followed by Korean and Indian ones. That has helped the lab's own supply chain. They did not break it down by item. The point was general: once China joined the supply chain, everything became cheaper and more affordable. The Chula team added that drugs which used to be very expensive to make are now affordable for Thai patients because of Chinese supply.
  • Outside evidence points the same way. Chinese drug makers have been switching to local reagent suppliers on cost and delivery time. In 2025, Shanghai Titan Scientific's shares rose about 54% while Thermo Fisher's fell about 8% (Reuters, August 2025). China's reagent imports slipped to $5.76bn in 2024 from $5.83bn in 2023.
  • Read-through: price pressure on the US tools makers (TMO, DHR, A, WAT, BRKR, RVTY), first in academic labs, where budgets are tight and switching is easiest. Emerging-market buyers such as Thailand will default to Chinese supply on price.
  • What limits it: regulated work (GMP manufacturing, clinical testing) is slow to switch suppliers. Instruments lock in years of consumables. The BIOSECURE Act restricts federally funded work with Chinese "biotechnology companies of concern", and tariffs raise the landed cost of Chinese kit.
  • Our view: this is a structural risk to the lab-tools names in the AI Science basket. It is a reason to prefer recurring, regulated revenue (bioprocessing, QC) over academic instrument sales.

6. Gene therapy for longevity is a hot new area. Context, no listed pure play.

  • What she said: gene therapy aimed at longevity is drawing attention.
  • This lines up with Retro Bio, OpenAI's protein-design partner, which works on reprogramming cells to a younger state. Retro is private.
  • Read-through: no direct basket name. Indirect demand for DNA (TWST) and for cell and gene manufacturing tools (DHR, TMO).

7. Personal "gene bots" and the guardrail problem. A watch item.

  • Who raised it: an audience member who works in biotech investing and recently met teams in Boston building personalised AI agents around a person's genes. These are early and private, not yet on the market. The risk is the guardrail: how do you know the bot's interest is aligned with yours, and can you trust what it tells you?
  • Read-through: for the data owners (NTRA, TEM), trust and regulation are the gate between genomic data and consumer AI. It is also a reminder that regulators will scrutinise AI in health. No listed exposure yet.

What changes in our view

  • Stronger: the bottleneck names. Monkeys, wet-lab capacity and labour are the scarce inputs, said by someone who buys them.
  • New: China as a cheaper equipment and materials supplier. We add it as a risk to the lab-tools names and to the academic share of revenue at A, BRKR, WAT and TMO.
  • Tempered: the speed of AI-led discovery. Human verification is still the rate limit, which supports Phase II as the real test (2028-30) and keeps AI drug developers an option, not a core holding.

Beyond investment: science for the developing world

The second theme of the evening was how to make these medicines reach countries like Thailand. Penn's lab works with the Gates Foundation on HIV and malaria vaccines for Africa, and with Chulalongkorn on Thailand's own mRNA vaccine. Both speakers made the same point from opposite ends: cheaper Chinese supply is what turns expensive Western science into medicine poorer countries can afford. That is a social good, and it is also the reason the China pricing risk in point 5 is structural rather than cyclical.

Open questions

  • On China pricing: which items and how large the gap is. Not covered at the talk.
  • On AI: which models the labs use, and for which tasks. Not named at the talk.
  • On autonomous labs: vendors and timelines. Not discussed.
