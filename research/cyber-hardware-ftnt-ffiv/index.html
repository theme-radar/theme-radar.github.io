Cybersecurity hardware: Fortinet and F5

Both names fell about 20% on earnings in the same week. The moat is being physically in the data path inside the customer's building, which AI cannot vibe-code. Own the category at equal weight, but wait: the entry conditions and kill triggers are set out name by name.

Tickers: FTNT, FFIV

Basket: Cybersecurity hardware. FTNT and FFIV at equal weight, waiting for an entry point.

Both names fell about 20% on earnings last week. FFIV fell 20.4% on 28-Oct and 26.3% from the 27-Oct close to the 30-Oct close. FTNT fell 20.1% on 29-Oct (it opened 26.5% down) and closed at $136.08 on 30-Oct, 30.1% below its 9-Oct peak.

I want to own both, equal weight, as exposure to the cybersecurity hardware category. I am not picking a winner between them. I am waiting for an entry point, and the conditions I want to see are set out at the end.

On a fundamental basis, both will benefit from the cybersecurity trend. Valuations are lower, but not uniformly cheap. On 30-Oct closes FFIV trades at 18.6x forward earnings and 5.2x EV/sales. FTNT trades at 36.3x and 12.2x.

Fortinet and F5 have one key advantage: their businesses are anchored in physical infrastructure, making them harder for frontier AI labs to disrupt. You can vibe-code software, but you can’t vibe-code a box or a chip into existence.

The downside is greater exposure to hardware spending cycles. That helps explain F5’s lower multiples against software-centric peers like Cloudflare (189.9x forward P/E, 39.5x EV/sales) and Zscaler (48.5x, 11.1x). For Fortinet it only partly holds. FTNT is cheaper than Zscaler on forward P/E but dearer on EV/sales, and it is far above FFIV on both.

I believe cybersecurity hardware deserves a place in a thematic cyber allocation. The moat isn’t the hardware itself. It’s being physically in the data path inside the customer’s own building. Given the current cybersecurity environment, every company will need that. The two names do different jobs in that data path (Figure 1), so owning both gives me the category rather than a bet on one company.

What AI cannot easily replicate

  • Installed position. Banks and governments route customer requests through BIG-IP. Ripping out the front door of a trading app is a career risk for a CIO, however cheap the alternative code is.

  • Accumulated configuration. Banks have years of custom iRules (BIG-IP’s traffic scripts) and policies. AI can help translate them, but someone still has to test and own the migration.

  • Physical custody. Sovereignty and regulation increasingly require the control point to sit in a facility the customer owns. A frontier lab can’t ship a certified appliance into a central bank’s data centre.

  • Throughput at line rate. Decrypting traffic cheaply at line rate needs purpose-built silicon (F5’s FPGA logic, Fortinet’s ASICs). That’s a supply-chain and silicon advantage, not a coding one. One caveat: systems were only $923m of F5’s $3,417m FY26 revenue (27%), so the silicon moat covers a minority of F5’s business.

What AI does erode

  • The software itself is getting cheaper to write. Open-source proxies like Envoy and HAProxy, and new entrants, will close feature gaps faster. F5 itself says that in the last three months AI helped it implement engineering fixes on its own code base that would previously have taken seven years of work. Cuts both ways.

  • Rule-writing is commoditising. Signature and rule production used to be a vendor’s job. The WAF that adapts by itself (F5’s AI WAF, Cloudflare’s equivalents) becomes table stakes, not a differentiator.

  • Hardware isn’t the only route into the data path. Cloudflare and Akamai sit there without a box. Their moat is a global network that is also impossible to vibe-code. So “in the data path” can be won without hardware.

Compare and contrast FTNT vs F5

FTNT sweet spot. Hospitals, factories, universities, retail chains: the left side is huge. Thousands of devices on campus (scanners, infusion pumps, production-line controllers, cameras) plus many sites to connect. That’s Fortinet’s sweet spot: firewalls between network zones, industrial security, and SD-WAN to link branches.

F5 sweet spot. Banks, brokers, airlines, e-commerce, government portals: the right side is huge, because the business is the website and the app. That’s F5’s sweet spot. Their pitch since 2025 has been that a bank with apps in its own data centres, in AWS and at the edge should write one security policy, not three.

The PHP flaws. The event is php2shell: two critical remote-code-execution flaws in PHP (CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619), disclosed and fixed on 21-Oct-2026. Cloudflare had WAF rules live about 9 hours before disclosure, including for free plans. In my opinion, the primary beneficiaries of events like this are the edge players, Cloudflare ($NET), Akamai ($AKAM) and Fastly ($FSLY), because a cloud WAF can push one rule to every customer at once. To be fair, F5’s CEO cited the same event on the 27-Oct call as a win for F5’s AI WAF, and Ken Xie also cited the PHP vulnerabilities on Fortinet’s call. My view is that F5 is trying to grow into this market but for now mainly serves its own installed base.

The service annuity: is the installed base still paying?

First, how these companies make money

Both companies earn money in two ways, and the two behave very differently.

  • Product (the box). A one-off sale of a hardware appliance: Fortinet’s FortiGate firewall, F5’s BIG-IP. It is booked as revenue in the quarter it ships. It is lumpy, because customers only replace boxes every few years. This is what drives the hardware cycle.

  • Service (the annuity). The subscriptions and support that keep the box useful: threat-intelligence updates, software updates, technical support. Customers pay for as long as the box is in use. It is booked as revenue evenly over the contract, slice by slice.

Why the split matters. Service is the better business. It repeats, it is predictable, and it carries much higher gross margin than hardware (Fortinet’s GAAP product gross margin is 64.4%, and its service margin is well above that). So product drives revenue growth and the cycles, while service drives gross profit, cash flow stability and the valuation multiple.

One quirk at F5. F5 reports “product” as systems (hardware) plus software. Part of that software is recurring (term subscriptions and SaaS), but accounting books much of a term licence’s value up front, so it lands in product. That makes F5’s headline service/product ratio look less recurring than the business really is, which is why I show F5 two ways below.

The ratio: service ÷ product

What it measures. For every $1 of new boxes sold this year, how many dollars of service the whole installed base paid. 2.0x means $2 of service for every $1 of boxes.

How to read a change:

  • Ratio rising: service is growing faster than box sales. The business is becoming more recurring (or hardware sales are slow).

  • Ratio falling because product boomed: not bad in itself. Many new boxes went out, and each should bring service revenue over the next few years. The ratio should climb back as they start paying.

  • Ratio falling because service weakened: bad. Customers are buying less service per box, or not renewing.

Both ratios fell in the latest year. In both cases product boomed, service did not shrink. The question for 2027–28 is whether the boom-year boxes bring the usual amount of service with them.

In plain terms:

  • FTNT, 2.12x to about 1.71x. Fortinet sold far more boxes in 2026 (product about +36% at the guide midpoint) while service grew about 13%. The base is still paying, but service is not keeping pace with the boom.

  • F5 headline, 1.21x to 0.91x. Systems grew 31% in each of FY25 and FY26 while services grew about 3%. Same story: a hardware refresh outran the annuity.

  • F5 with software counted as recurring, 3.34x to 2.30x. Still well above 1x, so most of F5’s revenue is recurring. It fell for the same reason, the hardware refresh.

The service revenue you see this quarter is the slow release of contracts signed in the past (Figure 2). So there are two stages to watch.

  • Leading indicators (signed or invoiced, not yet earned). These tell you what service revenue should look like over the next one to three years. Billings is what was invoiced this period (revenue plus the change in deferred revenue). Deferred revenue is cash invoiced for service not yet delivered. It sits on the balance sheet and moves into revenue month by month. RPO (remaining performance obligations) is all contracted revenue not yet recognised, invoiced or not. It is the broadest backlog measure.

  • Conversion (earned). Service revenue on the income statement. This is what actually reaches gross profit.

Normally, when billings and deferred revenue grow fast, service revenue speeds up a few quarters later. If the leading indicators stay strong but service revenue does not speed up, something is leaking. Two common causes: longer contracts (a 5-year deal invoiced up front inflates billings but spreads revenue thinly), or less service sold per box, which the industry calls lower “attach”.

  • Fortinet: the pipeline is full, but it is turning into revenue more slowly. The leading indicators are healthy: service billings +26% in Q3-26, deferred revenue +19.3%, RPO +18.8%. Conversion is not: service revenue grew only about 13%, down from 28.1% in FY23, and the FY26 service guide was cut again in October. The CFO blamed mix. Sovereign and networking deals carry less service per dollar of hardware. Why it matters: service is Fortinet’s high-margin, recurring profit engine. If attach is structurally lower, every new box is worth less over its life, and the stock deserves a lower multiple. The test: FY27 service growth above about 13%, which management says it will deliver.

  • F5: stable, but barely growing. Services grew 2.8% in FY26 and are guided to low to mid single digits for FY27. The leading indicators are better: deferred revenue +10.3% to $2,204.4m, RPO $2.2bn at Jun-26, term subscriptions +14% on “strong renewal rates”. Why it matters: F5’s growth in the last two years came from a one-off hardware refresh (systems +31% in each of FY25 and FY26). When the refresh ends, software subscriptions have to carry growth. The base is not leaking (2.30x with software counted as recurring). It is just not growing much.

Growth and AI, side by side

The rows below split the same revenue in different ways. Quick definitions:

  • Revenue growth: total sales booked in the period, product plus service.

  • Billings growth: what was invoiced. It runs ahead of revenue because multi-year subscriptions are invoiced up front and earned later (Figure 2). Fortinet discloses it, F5 does not. About 10 points of Fortinet’s Q3 billings growth came from price rises, not more units.

  • Hardware growth: the box line. Fortinet calls it “product” (mainly FortiGate appliances). F5 calls it “systems” (BIG-IP hardware). F5’s separate software line is not in this row.

  • Service growth: subscriptions and support from the installed base, the annuity.

  • FCF / non-GAAP net income: how much reported profit turns into cash. Above 100% means cash runs ahead of profit, usually because customers prepay multi-year subscriptions. That is Fortinet’s 146%. F5’s weak Q4 cash reflects an inventory build and supplier prepayments.

  • Disclosed AI metric: whether the company puts a number on AI demand at all.

The read: Fortinet grows faster on every line and converts more profit into cash. F5 is the only one that puts a number on AI. Neither has shown that AI revenue is recurring yet.

FTNT

Doc: FTNT primer

  • Hardware is peaking, not breaking. Product revenue grew 37% in Q3. IR said about 10 percentage points of billings growth came from price rises. Cheap inventory (components, memory) is used up: the CFO says inventory is “normal” from Q3 and lead times are getting longer. GAAP product gross margin fell from 67.7% to 64.4%. Q4 billings are guided to +20% at the midpoint, against +29% actual growth in Q3.

  • Software and services lag. Service revenue grew 13% and the full-year service guide was cut, to $5.15bn to $5.17bn from $5.18bn to $5.22bn. This is the third service-guide cut since Aug-2025. In July the CFO had said service growth would pick up in the second half.

  • The real worry is attach. New sovereign, neocloud and large-enterprise deals carry less service per dollar of hardware. The CFO said “Sovereign has less services” and conceded more discounting between hardware and services.

  • Rivals in cloud security are pulling ahead. Gartner moved Fortinet from Leader to Challenger in its Magic Quadrant for SASE Platforms, published 28-Jul-2026. That is three months old, not new news. There are four Leaders: Netskope, Cato Networks, Palo Alto Networks and Zscaler. Fortinet is still a Leader in the 2026 Magic Quadrant for Hybrid Mesh Firewall. Fortinet now reports a merged “SASE Firewall” pillar, which grew 34% in Q2 and 31% in Q3. Unified SASE billings grew 35% in Q2. AI-driven security operations billings growth slowed to 15% from 25% in one quarter.

  • Test: the 2027 service guide, due with the Q4 print in early February 2027 (estimated, last year it was 5-Feb-2026). The CFO has already said 2027 service growth will be higher than in 2026.

My FTNT case hinges on 1) hardware continuing to deliver growth and 2) services sales inflecting. Management cites timing (“It’s really a timing issue”), not market share loss, as the services headwind. But it also conceded mix, so I want to see the inflection before building exposure to FTNT. The upcoming $ANT IPO (Anthropic filed its S-1 on 26-Oct-2026, no date set) and this sector’s historical pattern are reasons to be patient. The valuation is more attractive than it was a month ago. I can wait for the mid-terms, the ANT IPO and the next prints, and see how the stock trades.

What history tells us

The point in one line: Fortinet’s big earnings-day crashes have been about when customers buy boxes, not about customers leaving. The stock has always recovered, usually within a year.

Four patterns:

  • Crashes are about hardware timing. Every big drop I checked (2016, 2023, 2025, 2026) came from a hardware or billings slowdown. In none of them did Fortinet lose its installed base. (Four other 10%+ earnings-day falls, in 2015, 2018 and twice in 2022, have causes I have not checked.)

  • Most of the damage happens on day one. The first day is usually well over half the total fall. The exception is when management cuts guidance a second time, as in 2023. Then the stock kept sliding for another three months.

  • Recovery takes months, not years. Usually 4 to 13 months back to the pre-crash price. The outlier is 2015, which took about two years.

  • Services never drove a recovery. The rebounds came from margins and buybacks (2023–24) or a new product cycle (2025–26), on my reading. Service growth has slowed the whole time.

What it means for today:

  • Base case is 2025. Same setup: billings guide raised, service guide cut, stock down about 22% in a day. It got back to the pre-crash price in 9 months and roughly doubled within a year.

  • Bear case is 2023. It applies only if Fortinet cuts its billings guide, which it has not done. Then expect a second leg down, about a third off the peak in total, and about a year to recover.

  • Where today is worse than 2025. The stock starts from a higher valuation (about 36x forward earnings vs about 28x at end-FY25, per secondary data). And part of this year’s hardware boom comes from price rises and cheap inventory running out, which looks like 2022–23.

  • So the one signal to watch is the billings guide. Held or raised points to the 2025 path. Cut points to the 2023 path.

The FY27 starting guide of 8–9% is higher than last year’s starting point, but last year’s start was depressed by the Oct-2025 breach. The FY25 start was 4–5%. The ladder also did not protect the stock. FFIV fell 20.4% on 28-Oct, as hard as FTNT, and kept sliding to 26.3% down by the 30-Oct close. The market sold the margin and EPS guide: gross margin down to 80.5–82.5% from 84.0% on component costs, EPS up only 3–5%, and H2 replacement bookings expected down. The “hardware peaking, services lag” critique I aimed at FTNT fits F5 too. In FY26 systems grew 31% and services 3%.

In the most recent call, management highlighted three direct AI use cases:

  • AI data delivery. In one APAC conglomerate example, BIG-IP is “the single high throughput control point for data moving between storage and GPU compute”. Customers deployed 29.2 Tb of F5-powered AI data delivery capacity in Q4 alone, up nearly 250% YoY, led by banking, financial services and healthcare. That is capacity deployed, not revenue. F5’s direct AI bookings were “well over $100 million” for FY26. (Peers: storage vendors’ own front ends, NetScaler.)

  • AI runtime security. When an enterprise exposes an AI model or agent, attackers try prompt injection, jailbreaks and data exfiltration. One of the world’s largest financial institutions used F5 Bot Defense to block personal AI agents logging into customer accounts at the front door. Several other large enterprises, not named as banks, do the same. These are outside agents arriving at the bank’s front door, which is exactly where F5 sits. The risk is that F5 is up against bundlers like PANW, CSCO and CRWD, which all want to sell into this layer.

  • AI factory load balancing. Inside an AI data centre, inference requests must be routed to the GPU that can answer fastest, with tenants isolated from each other. (Competitors: NVDA’s own software, open-source inference gateways and hyperscaler native stacks.)

Whether this AI demand turns into recurring revenue cannot be determined from F5’s disclosures. I will watch deferred revenue, RPO and term subscriptions rather than the bookings headline.

Trade-offs

  • Equal weight reduces single-name risk. FTNT’s risk is service conversion and SASE share. F5’s is the end of a refresh cycle and margin pressure. Owning both means neither alone decides the outcome.

  • It does not reduce sector risk. Both fell about 20% on earnings in the same week (FTNT -20.1%, FFIV -20.4%), and FTNT also fell 5.9% on 28-Oct, the day after F5 reported, before its own print. Both depend on hardware cycles (FTNT product +37%, F5 systems +31%), and component costs are squeezing both margin guides.

  • Equal weight in dollars is not equal valuation risk. FTNT trades at about twice F5’s forward P/E (36.3x vs 18.6x) and more than twice its EV/sales (12.2x vs 5.2x).

Entry plan and kill triggers

I would build exposure when I see the conditions below. These are what I am waiting for, not price targets. The thresholds are drawn from the company guides, and they are my framing, not company targets.

FTNT: what I want to see before building

  • Management comments on the service trajectory at the BMO (12-Nov), UBS (2-Dec) and Barclays (9-Dec) conferences.

  • Q4 billings at or above the guide, which keeps Aug-2025 as the analogue rather than Aug-2023.

  • A 2027 service guide above 2026’s about 12.6%, as the CFO has pre-signalled.

Kill triggers: Q4 billings below the $2.775bn low end, any cut to the billings guide or FY27 billings outlook, a 2027 service guide not above about 12.6%, or SecOps billings growth below 15%.

FFIV: what I want to see before building

  • Q1 FY27 results (late January 2027, estimated) showing the H1 systems refresh holding, revenue within $875m to $895m and gross margin within 81.5% to 82.5%.

  • Cash conversion recovering from Q4’s $91m FCF as the inventory build and supplier prepayments unwind.

Kill triggers: Systems growth decelerating faster than “upper single digit”, gross margin below the 80.5% low end, or a new exploited BIG-IP vulnerability tied to the source code stolen in the Oct-2025 breach.

Both: category checks

Kill trigger: evidence that php2shell-style events push WAF spend to edge vendors rather than appliances. Cloudflare’s print on 5-Nov is the first test.
